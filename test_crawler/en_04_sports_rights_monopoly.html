Polsat's sports rights lock-up: how one broadcaster turned the national passion into a subscription toll road

Polsat and its Polsat Box platform hold exclusive Polish rights to the Premier League, Formula 1, Serie A, and the UEFA Champions League. Fans who want to watch live sport legally now have no realistic option below PLN 80 per month. UOKiK has received four complaints. Nobody has acted.

Author: Sports Media deskDate: April 27, 2026Reading time: 6 min

For most of post-communist Polish broadcasting history, major international sport was accessible on free-to-air television. The Premier League, Formula 1, and Champions League were carried on channels that any household with an aerial or basic cable could receive. That era ended quietly — not through a single decision, but through a decade of rights acquisitions by Cyfrowy Polsat CPS and its Polsat Box platform that systematically shifted live sport behind a paywall. The price of admission in 2026: a minimum of PLN 79.99 per month for a Polsat Box subscription — or a Plus mobile contract at equivalent cost — to watch any of the four marquee competitions live.

The economics of sports rights acquisition are straightforward: exclusive rights are a subscriber acquisition tool. Polsat pays a premium to lock out competitors and compel fans with no alternative to subscribe. The strategy is not unique to Poland — Sky in the UK, DAZN across Europe, and ESPN DIS in the United States have built entire business models on it. What makes the Polish case distinctive is the degree of concentration: one operator, Cyfrowy Polsat, holds exclusive rights to virtually every major live sporting competition that Polish fans care about.

When one company controls access to football, Formula 1, and the Champions League, it does not have sports rights — it has a sports tax on every fan who refuses to go without.

UOKiK, Poland's competition regulator, has received at least four formal complaints since 2024 alleging that the concentration of sports rights in a single operator constitutes an abuse of market dominance harming consumer welfare. The regulator confirmed receipt of the complaints. Beyond that, there has been no public action, no investigation announcement, and no interim guidance. Meanwhile, CPS renewed its Formula 1 deal through 2029 in January 2026, extending its grip on the calendar before any regulatory challenge could mature. The speed of the renewal — and the silence from UOKiK — suggests the regulator either lacks the tools or the appetite to intervene in broadcast rights markets. Either answer should trouble Polish sports fans.

European precedents worth watching

Germany: The Bundeskartellamt (Federal Cartel Office) requires that no single broadcaster may hold more than 50% of Bundesliga broadcast rights. A structural model Poland has never adopted.

Italy: AGCM investigated exclusive Serie A rights bundling in 2023. Settlement required partial sub-licensing to free-to-air platforms for highlight packages — a minimum access floor Polsat faces no equivalent obligation to provide.

EU Digital Markets Act: Legal scholars at Kozminski University Warsaw argue the DMA's interoperability provisions may, if applied creatively, open a pathway to challenge exclusive broadcast rights concentration. The European Commission has not confirmed this interpretation.
