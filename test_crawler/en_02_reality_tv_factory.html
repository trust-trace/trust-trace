Polsat's reality TV factory: how Poland's second-largest broadcaster industrialised stupidity and called it entertainment

Big Brother. Bar. Love Island. Ninja Warrior. Polsat has built the most prolific reality TV assembly line in Central European broadcasting history — and systematically dismantled the journalism infrastructure it replaced. This is not an accident. It is a business model.

Author: Culture & Media deskDate: April 27, 2026Reading time: 7 min

Scroll through Polsat's weekly schedule and you will find a broadcaster that has made a clear and deliberate editorial choice: reality television is the product, news is the scheduling obligation. Big Brother, Bar, Love Island Polska, Ninja Warrior, Twoja Twarz Brzmi Znajomo — the flagship prime-time slots go to formats whose selection criteria for participants prioritise photogenic conflict over any other human quality. The news desk survives in a narrow window around 18:50, flanked by game shows on both sides.

The commercial logic is straightforward: reality television is cheap to produce relative to its ratings yield. A season of Bar costs a fraction of maintaining a network of foreign correspondents. A Love Island franchise requires no expertise, no sources, and generates controversy that is algorithmically friendly on social media. For a broadcaster competing for advertising revenue in an environment where Netflix NFLX and YouTube are taking digital budgets, the reality format is a cost-efficient defensive play.

When a broadcaster with a national audience of millions decides that six hours of weekly news is sufficient, it has made a political statement — whether it intends to or not.

The cultural cost is harder to quantify but no less real. Polsat reaches an estimated 12–14 million viewers weekly across its channel family. That is a significant fraction of the Polish adult population exposed to a media diet where investigative journalism has been reduced to half an hour per week and the most sophisticated editorial decision of the day is which contestant to evict. Media researchers at Jagiellonian University published a 2025 study linking heavy commercial TV consumption with measurably lower political knowledge scores among 18–35-year-old viewers — the demographic Polsat's programming most aggressively targets.

Polsat is not legally obligated to provide more journalism than it does — it operates under a commercial broadcasting licence, not a public service mandate. But the Polish Broadcasting Act does require licensees to serve the public interest. Whether seven simultaneously airing reality formats and 0.5 hours of documentary content per week constitutes public interest broadcasting is a question KRRiT has conspicuously avoided asking.
