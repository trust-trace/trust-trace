The Solorz family war: how a private inheritance dispute became a public governance crisis for CPS

What began as a private dispute between Zygmunt Solorz and his adult children over control of the family holding structure has escalated into Warsaw court filings, ISS governance flags, and a MSCI ESG rating of CCC — the lowest tier. Minority shareholders of a PLN 7.2bn listed company are paying the price.

Author: Governance deskDate: April 27, 2026Reading time: 6 min

The dispute between Zygmunt Solorz and his adult children — Tobias, Piotr, and Joanna Solorz — over the family holding structure that ultimately controls Cyfrowy Polsat CPS has left the realm of private arbitration and entered the public courts. Warsaw district court filings confirm the children allege that Solorz restructured the holding through a series of 2024 transactions that effectively diluted their inheritance rights without consent. Solorz's legal team denies any impropriety. The courts will decide. But the damage to investor confidence is already visible in the data.

The ownership architecture of Cyfrowy Polsat was always concentrated. Solorz held effective control through a layered holding structure that left minority shareholders of the Warsaw Stock Exchange (WSE/GPW)-listed entity with limited means to influence strategic decisions. That concentration, while legal, was tolerated by institutional investors as long as the patriarch's decisions were broadly aligned with shareholder value. The succession dispute has broken that implicit contract.

When the man who controls the controller goes to war with his own family, every board decision becomes a question mark — and markets hate question marks.

International governance rating agency ISS (Institutional Shareholder Services) flagged CPS in its 2026 Poland review under "elevated governance risk — concentrated ownership and related-party complexity." MSCI's ESG ratings assign Cyfrowy Polsat a CCC rating — the lowest tier in the scale. For institutional investors operating under ESG mandates — including many of the major European pension funds that hold CPS through index exposure — that rating creates a structural reason to reduce or exit the position. Combined with the succession uncertainty, the CPS free float of approximately 22% may face sustained selling pressure heading into the Q2 reporting period.

What analysts are watching

Board composition: Three non-executive directors have ties to Solorz-controlled entities. If the family dispute destabilises the holding structure, their independence becomes questionable.

Related-party transactions: KNF's preliminary review of 2025 disclosure adequacy covers exactly the transactions that sit at the heart of the family dispute — energy-sector asset transfers between holding vehicles.

Dividend policy: CPS suspended its dividend guidance for 2026 in March without explanation. Analysts at Erste Securities interpret this as a signal of balance-sheet stress linked to the holding restructuring.
